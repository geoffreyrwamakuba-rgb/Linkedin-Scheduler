Query plans used to be something you argued with. On a modern warehouse they are closer to something you read afterwards.

→ Postgres still shows you everything. Run EXPLAIN ANALYZE and every node carries the estimated row count next to the actual one. When the optimizer expected 5 rows and got 4,800, the bad guess is sitting on the line that caused it. Then you can overrule the decision: force a different join, rewrite the predicate, add the index it wanted.

→ Snowflake deliberately does not. There are no indexes to pick and no join hints to force. You get a profile after the query has already run, and two numbers carry most of the diagnosis. Partitions scanned against partitions total tells you whether pruning worked, and a ratio near 100% means your filter skipped nothing. Bytes spilled to remote storage tells you the warehouse was too small for what you asked it to do.

→ Spark sits in the middle, and it is the interesting one. df.explain() prints a plan without running anything, so it cannot know what the data really looks like. That is why the first line of the output says AdaptiveSparkPlan isFinalPlan=false. Once the query runs, Adaptive Query Execution rewrites it from real shuffle statistics: coalescing small partitions, switching sort-merge joins to broadcast joins, splitting skewed tasks into even ones. The flag only reads true once it has finished.

So the plan you read is often not the plan that ran.

On a warehouse that leaves you three levers: how the data is laid out, how much compute you gave it, and whether your filter can prune at all.

Most of the tuning moved out of the plan and into the data underneath it.
