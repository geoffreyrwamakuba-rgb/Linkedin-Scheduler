Somewhere upstream of your warehouse there is a spreadsheet a person edits by hand, and a pipeline that breaks when they do.

It has been load-bearing for two years, with no schema and nobody owning it on paper. Everybody knows about it. The instinct is to delete it.

That usually fails. The sheet exists for a reason the warehouse does not cover: someone has to enter a judgement no system produces, an override, a mapping, a rate agreed in a meeting. Delete it and the dependency just moves somewhere you cannot see.

What makes it different from every other upstream source is that the producer is a person, not a service.

→ You cannot ask them to publish a schema, version an interface or announce a breaking change. Whatever you build has to work without their cooperation, because they do not think of themselves as running a system.

→ So validate at the boundary. Column names, types, row counts and the two or three business rules that matter, checked at ingestion, before anything downstream touches it.

→ Then fail toward the person who changed it. An engineer can patch a renamed column in five minutes, and that is the trap: every quiet fix teaches the producer that nothing downstream depends on them, so the next change arrives unannounced too.

→ Renames are the easy half anyway. The case that hurts is a column that keeps its name and changes its meaning, net where it used to be gross. The shape still validates and every number downstream is wrong.

→ So freeze the shape. One named owner, one tab nothing else writes to, and three rules: add columns at the end, never rename, never reorder.

The worst time to find out the shape changed is month end, when the person who owns the sheet is busiest and least likely to mention it.
